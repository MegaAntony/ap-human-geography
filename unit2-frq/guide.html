What is an FRQ? The 4 Golden Rules

In 9th grade, many students think an FRQ (Free-Response Question) is an English essay. It is NOT. If you try to write a five-paragraph essay with an introduction and conclusion, you will waste valuable time and receive zero extra points.

What NOT to do:

  • ❌ No Introductions or Conclusions: Never write "Throughout history, humans have moved..." Just start directly with Part A.
  • ❌ No Bullet Points: The College Board requires complete sentences. Bullet points get an automatic zero.
  • ❌ No Vague Language: Don't say "people died because of sickness." Say "Crude Death Rate spiked due to infectious waterborne cholera."

What to DO to get 7/7:

  • ✔ Label Every Part: Write Part A:, Part B:, Part C: down the left side of your paper.
  • ✔ Obey the Task Verbs: If it says Identify, write 1 sentence. If it says Explain, connect cause and effect.
  • ✔ Use the "Because / This leads to" Rule: College Board AP readers look for the words because, which results in, and therefore to award explanation points.
02

The 3 College Board Task Verbs Mastered

Task Verb 1 1 Point

IDENTIFY

What it means: Simply name, state, or point out a specific geographic feature, country, or DTM stage.

How to write: 1 complete, direct sentence. Do NOT waste time explaining "why."
"Country X is in Stage 2 of the Demographic Transition Model."
Task Verb 2 1 Point

DESCRIBE

What it means: Provide the physical characteristics, appearance, or attributes. What does it look like?

How to write: 2 complete sentences illustrating the pattern or structure.
"Stage 2 of the DTM is characterized by a high Crude Birth Rate and a plummeting Crude Death Rate, creating an expansive pyramid with a very wide youth base."
Task Verb 3 1 Point

EXPLAIN

What it means: Show the how, why, or consequence. You must establish a clear cause-and-effect relationship.

How to write: 2–4 sentences using the word "because" and "which results in".
"The Crude Death Rate declines rapidly in Stage 2 because modern antibiotics and water sanitation reduce infant mortality, which leads to explosive population increase."

Population Pyramids & Dependency Ratios

A **population pyramid** (age-sex composition graph) displays male cohorts on the left and female cohorts on the right in 5-year age intervals. When looking at a pyramid on your test, check the **base** (young kids aged 0–14) and the **apex** (seniors aged 65+).

Expansive Pyramid (Stage 2)

e.g., Nigeria, Uganda
  • • Visual Shape: Wide triangle base, very steep taper toward the top.
  • • Demographic Meaning: High Crude Birth Rate (CBR) and high Total Fertility Rate (TFR). Lots of children being born.
  • • Dependency Ratio: High Youth Dependency Ratio. A huge percentage of children under 15 rely on a smaller working-age workforce (ages 15–64).
  • • Economic Strain: The government must spend heavily on primary schools, pediatric vaccines, and maternity wards, which takes money away from building highways and factories.

Constrictive Pyramid (Stage 4 or 5)

e.g., Japan, Italy, Germany
  • • Visual Shape: Inverted "beehive" or urn shape; narrow base with a bulge in middle and upper age cohorts (55+).
  • • Demographic Meaning: Total Fertility Rate has fallen below the 2.1 replacement level. Couples have fewer children.
  • • Dependency Ratio: High Elderly Dependency Ratio. A huge retired population aged 65+ relies on a shrinking workforce.
  • • Economic Strain: Severe labor shortages, shrinking tax revenues, and skyrocketing government costs for geriatric healthcare and public pensions (Social Security).
Key Math Formula for FRQs:
Dependency Ratio = (Population Aged 0–14 + Population Aged 65+) / (Working-Age Population Aged 15–64) × 100
04

The DTM & Epidemiological Transition (ETM)

The **Demographic Transition Model (DTM)** shows how population changes as a country develops economically. The **Epidemiological Transition Model (ETM)** explains the exact diseases causing death in each stage.

Stage Birth Rate (CBR) Death Rate (CDR) Natural Increase (NIR) Leading Causes of Death (ETM)
Stage 1: Low Growth Very High Very High Near Zero Pestilence & Famine (Black Plague, starvation, animal attacks)
Stage 2: High Growth Very High Plummets Rapidly Explosive Growth Receding Pandemics (Clean water, sewage systems, penicillin, vaccines)
Stage 3: Moderate Growth Drops Sharply Continues Falling Slowing Down Degenerative Diseases (Early cardiovascular disease, cancer)
Stage 4: Low Growth Low Low Zero Growth (ZPG) Delayed Degenerative Diseases (Heart bypass surgery, chemotherapy)
Stage 5: Decline Very Low (< CDR) Low to Moderate Negative Growth Reemergence of Infectious Diseases (Antibiotic resistance, MRSA)
💡 The #1 Most Tested Question on the DTM:

"Why does CDR fall before CBR in Stage 2?"
The Answer: Because medical technology (antibiotics, clean municipal water, pasteurization) can be imported and implemented immediately to stop children from dying. In contrast, cultural and religious customs that encourage large families take several generations to change!

05

Thomas Malthus vs. Ester Boserup (Food & Population)

Thomas Malthus (1798)

The Theory: Population grows exponentially/geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8, 16...), while food production only grows arithmetically/linearly (1, 2, 3, 4, 5...).

The Consequence: Society will eventually exceed its Carrying Capacity, triggering a "Malthusian Catastrophe" of widespread famine, disease, and war ("positive checks").

Neo-Malthusians Today: Argue Malthus was right, but on a broader scale: population growth is running out of clean water, arable soil, fossil fuels, and energy—not just wheat.

Ester Boserup & Critics (Counter-Theory)

The Theory: "Necessity is the mother of invention." As population grows and land becomes scarce, human ingenuity invents new technologies to increase agricultural yields.

Why Malthus Was Wrong: Malthus failed to foresee the **Agricultural & Green Revolutions**: tractors, synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, genetically modified seeds (GMOs), refrigeration, and containerized global cargo ships that exponentially increased food supply.

Migration, Distance Decay & Refugees

Distance Decay

As the distance between two places increases, the interaction between them drops. Migrants are far more likely to travel short distances across a shared border than cross an ocean.

Refugee

A person forced to flee their home due to armed conflict, violence, or persecution who has crossed an international border and is protected under UNHCR treaties.

IDP (Internally Displaced)

A person forced to flee their home due to the same armed conflict or disaster, but who remains within their own country's national borders (no international legal asylum).

Ravenstein's 5 Laws of Migration (High-Yield):
  1. Most migrants move only a short distance (Distance Decay).
  2. Long-distance migrants typically head to major urban centers of commerce and industry.
  3. Migration occurs in steps (Step Migration: farm ➔ village ➔ regional town ➔ major metropolis).
  4. Historically, most long-distance migrants are young single adult males rather than entire family units.
  5. Every migration flow produces a counterflow (return migration).
07

Dual Economic Consequences: Remittances vs. Brain Drain

Mrs. Knight loves asking about the economic impact of migration on the **origin country (sending country)**:

Positive Impact: Remittances ($)

Migrants working in developed nations wire cash back to their relatives in their homeland. Remittances inject direct funding into families for nutrition, private schooling, and healthcare, acting as a massive boost to the GDP of developing nations (e.g., Mexico, Philippines, Haiti).

Negative Impact: Brain Drain

The origin country loses its best and brightest. Doctors, software engineers, and university professors emigrate to wealthy nations for higher wages and research funding. This leaves the origin country with severe shortages of skilled doctors to staff local hospitals.

08

Pronatalist vs. Antinatalist Government Policies

Pronatalist Policies (Encourage Births)

Used by Stage 4 & 5 countries (e.g., France, Japan, Russia, South Korea) facing population decline and shrinking workforces.

  • • Direct cash baby bonuses for having children.
  • • Subsidized municipal pre-school and public daycare.
  • • Long paid maternal and paternal maternity leave.
  • • National tax discounts for large families.

Antinatalist Policies (Discourage Births)

Used by Stage 2 & 3 countries (e.g., China's historic One-Child Policy, India's sterilizations) facing runaway population growth exceeding food and water infrastructure.

  • • Fines and legal penalties for having more than the allowed children.
  • • Mandatory or subsidized female sterilization and birth control clinics.
  • • Nationwide public education billboards promoting small families.

Full 7/7 Mock In-Class FRQ Exam & Answer Key

Stimulus Context: You are given a map showing global refugee migrations from Syria to Europe and neighboring Middle Eastern countries between 2011 and 2020.

Part A (1 Point): IDENTIFY ONE political push factor that caused civilians to flee Syria.

Scoring Model: ONE political push factor is the armed violence and aerial barrel bombing carried out by the Syrian government during the civil war, which destroyed civilian infrastructure and threatened personal safety.

Part B (1 Point): DESCRIBE the geographic pattern of Syrian refugee settlement shown on the map.

Scoring Model: The vast majority of Syrian refugees settled in immediate neighboring nations across contiguous international borders (such as Turkey, Lebanon, and Jordan), with significantly smaller numbers traveling longer distances to Western European countries like Germany.

Part C (1 Point): EXPLAIN how the pattern described in Part B illustrates Ravenstein's laws of migration.

Scoring Model: This settlement pattern illustrates Ravenstein's law of distance decay because most migrants travel only short distances to seek immediate safety in bordering countries due to lower transportation costs and geographic proximity, while long-distance migration requires significantly more financial resources.

Part D (1 Point): DESCRIBE the legal distinction between a refugee and an internally displaced person (IDP).

Scoring Model: A refugee is an individual who has crossed an international border to escape persecution or armed conflict and receives legal protection under international law, whereas an internally displaced person has fled for the same reasons but remains within the national borders of their home country.

Part E (1 Point): EXPLAIN ONE economic challenge faced by receiving countries (such as Turkey or Lebanon) that accept large refugee populations.

Scoring Model: Receiving countries face fiscal strain because host governments must rapidly fund public refugee camps, provide emergency medical supplies, and build makeshift schools for refugee children, which places severe deficits on municipal budgets and public utility infrastructure.

Part F (1 Point): EXPLAIN ONE economic benefit that refugees can provide to destination countries in Western Europe.

Scoring Model: Refugees benefit European destination economies because they provide young, working-age labor in countries experiencing demographic aging (Stage 4 or 5 of the DTM), which fills critical worker shortages in manufacturing and service industries and contributes payroll taxes to fund social welfare programs.

Part G (1 Point): EXPLAIN ONE social or cultural challenge that can arise in destination countries receiving large influxes of international migrants.

Scoring Model: Rapid migration can spark social tension because differences in language, religious customs, and cultural practices can lead to residential segregation into ethnic enclaves, which can trigger localized anti-immigrant sentiment and xenophobia among native citizens who fear the loss of their cultural identity.
