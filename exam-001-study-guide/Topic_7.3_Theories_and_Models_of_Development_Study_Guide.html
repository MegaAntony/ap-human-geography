Rostow’s 5 Stages of Economic Growth (1960)

📜 Modernization Theory (W.W. Rostow)
Economist W.W. Rostow proposed a linear, five-stage structural model arguing that all countries can transition from traditional agrarian poverty to modern high-income prosperity through capital investment, technology diffusion, and industrialization (modeled after Western European and US history).
Stage of Growth Core Economic Characteristics Labor & Technology Focus Historical & Modern Examples
Stage 1: Traditional Society Subsistence agriculture; primary sector dominance; limited capital accumulation; trade is local or barter-based. Labor-intensive manual farming; static social hierarchy; minimal technological innovation. Medieval Europe; remote rural communities in South Sudan or Somalia today.
Stage 2: Preconditions for Takeoff Progressive leadership emerges; elite investment in vital national infrastructure (transportation canals/roads, water irrigation, ports, power grids). Shift toward commercial agriculture; extraction of raw natural resources for export to world markets. Great Britain in the early 1700s; contemporary resource exporters like Nigeria or Ghana.
Stage 3: Takeoff Rapid industrialization concentrated in a small number of leading manufacturing sectors (textiles, food processing). Capital investment exceeds $10\%$ of national income. Massive rural-to-urban migration into factory cities; technological breakthroughs in manufacturing; emergence of an entrepreneurial middle class. Britain during the Industrial Revolution (1780s–1820s); modern garment hubs like Bangladesh and Vietnam.
Stage 4: Drive to Maturity Modern technology diffuses across all economic sectors; industrial base diversifies into heavy industry, chemicals, steel, machinery, and automobiles. Rapid growth of tertiary transportation, banking, and commercial services; rising real wages and urbanization. United States in the early 1900s; contemporary emerging industrial powerhouses like China, Brazil, and India.
Stage 5: Age of High Mass Consumption High-income, consumer-driven economy; dominant tertiary, quaternary, and quinary sectors. Widespread consumer durable goods (cars, home appliances, smartphones); expanded social security/public welfare programs and high disposable income. United States, Japan, Germany, United Kingdom, and Canada today.

Major Critiques of Rostow’s Growth Model

1. Eurocentric / Western Bias

Assumes that all non-Western countries must follow the historical trajectory of Great Britain and the United States, ignoring distinct cultural, institutional, and environmental realities.

2. Linear Determinism Trap

Assumes progress is an inevitable, one-way upward ladder. In reality, countries can experience political collapse, civil war, debt defaults, or severe economic regression (e.g., Argentina, Venezuela).

3. Ignores Colonial Exploitation & Debt

Treats each nation as an isolated island. It ignores how centuries of European colonial resource extraction, neocolonial debt burdens, and unfair global trade terms prevent peripheral countries from reaching takeoff.

4. Environmental & Resource Limits

If all 8 billion people on Earth achieved Stage 5 High Mass Consumption at US/European levels of resource consumption and carbon emissions, Earth's planetary boundaries and ecosystems would collapse.

Wallerstein’s World Systems Theory (1974)

🌐 The Structuralist Global Capitalist System (Immanuel Wallerstein)
Unlike Rostow's national-level model, Wallerstein viewed the global economy as a single, integrated, hierarchical capitalist system based on an international division of labor. The system consists of three interconnected tiers: Core, Semi-Periphery, and Periphery.
Tier Economic Characteristics & Labor Role in the Global Economy Representative Countries
CORE High wealth, advanced technology, high education, high wages, dominant tertiary, quaternary, and quinary sectors. Controls global capital, banking, R&D, and multinational corporate headquarters. Exploits the periphery for cheap raw materials and low-wage assembly. United States, United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, Canada, Australia, France.
SEMI-PERIPHERY Intermediate levels of wealth and industrialization; mix of core and peripheral processes; major secondary manufacturing hubs. Serves as a global manufacturing buffer; exploits the periphery for raw materials while simultaneously being exploited by the core for cheap industrial manufacturing. China, India, Brazil, Mexico, South Africa, Vietnam, Turkey, Indonesia.
PERIPHERY Low wealth, low technology, low wages, low education, high infant mortality, dominant primary sector extraction. Structurally dependent on the core; exports unprocessed agricultural commodities, timber, and minerals; imports high-cost manufactured goods. Democratic Republic of Congo, Chad, Haiti, Afghanistan, Cambodia, Mali.
🔄 The Asymmetric Flow of Wealth in World Systems Theory
Periphery $\rightarrow$ Core: Cheap raw materials (copper, cobalt, cocoa, crude oil) and low-wage manual labor.
Core $\rightarrow$ Periphery: High-value manufactured consumer goods, intellectual property, financial loans, and capital machinery.

Multi-Scale Core-Periphery Dynamics

📐 Scale-Free Property of Core-Periphery
Core-Periphery relationships are not restricted to global international comparisons. They operate across multiple geographic scales of spatial analysis!
Global Scale

The Global North (MDC Core nations in North America and Western Europe) dominating international trade and financing over the Global South (LDC Periphery).

National / Regional Scale

Within Brazil, the affluent, industrialized Southeastern corridor around São Paulo acts as the national Core, while the rural Amazon Basin and drought-stricken Northeast act as the internal Periphery.

Local / Metropolitan Scale

A wealthy downtown financial central business district (CBD) or suburban tech corridor acts as the local Core, while underserved inner-city neighborhoods or isolated rural exurbs act as the local Periphery.

Dependency Theory & Commodity Dependence

⚠️ Core Concept: Dependency Theory
Dependency Theory states that the poverty and underdevelopment of peripheral countries is not an accidental stage of history, but the direct result of historical exploitation and unequal economic power structures maintained by wealthy core nations (neocolonialism).

Commodity Dependence (The Vulnerability Trap)

📊 The 60% UNCTAD Threshold
A country is officially classified as Commodity Dependent when primary raw commodities (e.g., crude petroleum, copper, coffee, cocoa, soybeans) account for more than 60% of its total merchandise export revenue.
Dangers of Commodity Dependence
  • Global Price Volatility: A sudden drop in world oil or copper prices immediately collapses national tax revenues and triggers currency devaluations.
  • The Resource Curse: Foreign oil/mining revenues often fuel corruption and crowd out investments in public education and healthcare.
  • Low Added Value: Exporting raw unrefined cacao yields pennies compared to importing processed Swiss chocolate bars.
The Solution: Beneficiation & Diversification
  • Beneficiation: Industrially processing raw materials domestically before export (e.g., refining bauxite into aluminum metal inside the country).
  • Economic Diversification: Developing secondary manufacturing and tourism/tech service sectors to reduce single-commodity reliance.

The UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

🌱 The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development
Adopted in 2015 by all 193 United Nations member states, the 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) provide a universal blueprint for achieving peace, human dignity, and environmental protection by 2030.

The SDGs are built on the Triple Bottom Line of sustainability: balancing Social Equity, Environmental Protection, and Economic Prosperity.

High-Yield SDG Focus Areas Specific Target & Objective Geographic Impact
SDG 1: No Poverty & SDG 2: Zero Hunger Eradicate extreme poverty (living on $<\$2.15/\text{day}$) and end hunger through sustainable agriculture and food security. Reduces rural malnutrition and famine vulnerability in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
SDG 3: Good Health & SDG 4: Quality Education End preventable maternal/child deaths and provide universal, equitable primary and secondary schooling. Drives the female literacy multiplier effect, lowering TFR and raising living standards.
SDG 5: Gender Equality End gender-based legal discrimination, empower women through land ownership, and expand microloans. Accelerates micro-enterprise growth and improves community nutrition and health.
SDG 6: Clean Water & SDG 7: Clean Energy Universal access to safe drinking water, sanitation, and affordable renewable solar/wind power. Combats waterborne pathogens (cholera) and reduces reliance on polluting indoor biomass fuels.
SDG 12: Responsible Consumption & SDG 13: Climate Action Decouple economic growth from environmental degradation and build climate resilience. Protects low-lying coastal populations and small island developing states (SIDS) from sea-level rise.

AP Exam Traps & Interactive Checkpoints

Q1: How does Wallerstein's World Systems Theory fundamentally differ from Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth?
Answer: Structural global exploitation vs. National linear progress. Rostow views development as a national, linear progression where every country can independently achieve mass consumption. Wallerstein views development as a global, structural hierarchy where the prosperity of Core nations relies upon the persistent exploitation of Periphery nations.
Q2: What is the primary vulnerability of a nation with commodity dependence (>60% of exports in raw materials)?
Answer: Extreme Global Price Volatility. When world commodity prices fall unexpectedly, the country's national revenue collapses, triggering severe budget deficits, currency depreciation, and foreign debt crises.
Q3: What characterizes Rostow's Stage 3 (Takeoff)?
Answer: Rapid industrialization in leading manufacturing sectors. Capital investment surges above 10% of national income, factories expand rapidly in cities (textiles, food processing), and massive rural-to-urban migration occurs.
Q4: Give an example of core-periphery dynamics operating at a national (sub-national) scale.
Answer: Brazil's Southeast Core vs. Amazonian Periphery. The wealthy industrial metropolis of São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro acts as Brazil's national economic Core, extracting raw timber, beef, and minerals from the underdeveloped Amazonian interior Periphery.
