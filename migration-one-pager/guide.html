What Exactly is Migration? (The Core Foundation)

Think about your daily routine: you wake up, ride the bus to school, go to soccer practice, and come home. Geographers call this circulation or mobility because it is short-term and repetitive. You are not moving your life.

Migration is fundamentally different: it is a permanent relocation to a new home in a new community or country.

Emigration (Exit)

Migration from a location. Think "E" for Exit. If you move away from Forsyth County to London, you are an emigrant from Georgia.

Immigration (Into)

Migration to a new location. Think "I" for Into. When you arrive in London, you are an immigrant to the United Kingdom.

The Net Migration Formula:
Net Migration = Immigrants − Emigrants
• If more people move in than move out: Positive Net Migration (Net In-Migration, like Georgia or Florida).
• If more people leave than arrive: Negative Net Migration (Net Out-Migration, like rural agricultural states or countries in conflict).

Push Factors vs. Pull Factors (Why People Pack Their Bags)

Nobody moves for no reason. Geographers explain every decision using a magnet metaphor:

  • Push Factors (The Bad Stuff): Negative conditions that induce people to move out of their present location (repelling them away).
  • Pull Factors (The Good Stuff): Positive conditions that induce people to move into a new location (attracting them in).

The 4 Major Buckets Tested on the AP Exam:

1. Economic Factors (#1 Cause Globally)

• Push: Job scarcity, low factory wages, high inflation, severe poverty.
• Pull: Booming tech industries, high-wage construction jobs, agricultural labor demand.
Example: Job seekers moving to Atlanta for corporate headquarters.

2. Political Factors

• Push: Violent dictatorships, armed civil war, state persecution, denial of free speech.
• Pull: Democracy, civil liberties, safety, rule of law, political asylum.
Example: Syrian families fleeing civil war to find sanctuary in Europe.

3. Environmental Factors

• Push: Harsh blizzards, prolonged droughts, desertification (Sahel), rising seas.
• Pull: Warm climate, scenic mountain regions, reliable access to clean water.
Example: Retirees moving to the warm Sunbelt (Florida, Arizona, Georgia).

4. Social & Cultural Factors

• Push: Religious or ethnic discrimination, rigid caste systems, gender oppression.
• Pull: Religious freedom, top universities, established family networks.
Example: Mormons migrating west to Utah in the 1840s for religious freedom.

Roadblocks vs. Detours: Intervening Obstacles & Opportunities

When people travel across the world, their journey is rarely a straight line on an empty highway. Along the way, two critical things can happen:

Intervening Obstacle (The Barrier)

An environmental or cultural/political feature of the landscape that hinders, delays, or stops migration.

  • Historically: Rough seas (Atlantic Ocean), scorching deserts (Sonoran Desert), snowy mountains (Alps, Rockies).
  • Modern Day: Visas, passports, quotas, border patrols, legal deportation walls.

Intervening Opportunity (The Detour)

A positive circumstance encountered on the journey that persuades a migrant to settle permanently before reaching their original planned destination.

  • Example: A family moving from Florida to New York stops in Atlanta, finds fantastic jobs and affordable rent, and decides to stay in Georgia permanently!

Forced vs. Voluntary: The Crucial Legal Distinctions

The AP Exam will aggressively test whether you know the difference between people who chose to move versus people who were forced to run for their lives:

The "Big Three" Humanitarian Definitions:

Refugee

Forced to flee their home and has crossed an INTERNATIONAL BORDER due to armed conflict, violence, or fear of persecution.

Protected by 1951 UN Convention.
IDP (Internally Displaced)

Forced to flee their home for the same reasons as a refugee, but REMAINS INSIDE THEIR COUNTRY'S BORDERS.

Does NOT have UN international legal rights.
Asylum Seeker

A migrant who arrived at a destination border and FORMALLY APPLIED for legal recognition as a refugee, waiting for court approval.

In legal processing limbo.

Three High-Yield Types of Voluntary Migration:

1. Chain Migration:

Moving to a specific town because family members, cousins, or friends from your home country moved there first.

2. Step Migration:

Long-distance movement completed in baby steps: Farm ➔ Village ➔ Small Town ➔ Big City.

3. Transhumance:

Seasonal cyclic migration of herders moving livestock up into mountains in summer and down into valleys in winter.

Ravenstein's 7 Laws of Migration & The Gravity Model

Back in 1885, an English geographer named E.G. Ravenstein analyzed British census records and discovered that people do not wander randomly. Human migration follows predictable spatial laws. You need at least 3 of these on your one-pager:

Law 1: Short Distances & Distance Decay: Most migrants travel only short distances within their own country. The further away a place is, the less likely someone will move there.
Law 2: Urban Gravitation: Long-distance migrants predominantly head toward major economic hubs and large metropolitan cities (The Gravity Model).
Law 3: Step Migration: Long-distance moves occur in successive stages or increments rather than all at once.
Law 4: Rural to Urban Flow: Historically, rural agricultural populations are far more likely to migrate than urban citizens.
Law 5: Counter-Streams: Every major migration flow produces a compensating counter-stream (e.g., return migration).
Law 6: Demographics: Historically, young single adult males migrate internationally for work, whereas families move internally.
Law 7: Economic Primacy: Economic opportunities are the number-one cause of all migration worldwide.
The Gravity Model Formula (Made Simple)

Spatial Interaction = (Population A × Population B) ÷ (Distance)²

What it means in plain English: Big cities act like giant magnets. Even if New York or Atlanta are far away, their huge population and job market overpower the distance friction and pull migrants from across the world!

Wilbur Zelinsky's Migration Transition Model

Geographer Wilbur Zelinsky noticed that a country’s migration patterns change directly along with its stage in the Demographic Transition Model (DTM):

Stage 1 (Pre-Industrial / Hunter-Gatherer):

High birth and death rates. High daily/seasonal mobility for food, but no permanent international migration.

Stage 2 (Rapid Growth / Overcrowded):

Death rates plummet; population explodes. Massive international emigration to other countries and internal rural-to-urban moves.

Stage 3 & 4 (Industrial & Modern Nations):

Destinations for international immigrants. Internal moves shift from cities out to suburbs (suburbanization).

Stage 5 (Declining Population / Graying):

Birth rates below death rates. Desperately need guest workers and immigrants to sustain the economy and pensions.

7

Geographic Consequences: Sending vs. Receiving Societies

Migration impacts both the place people leave and the place people arrive. On your one-pager, you need to show both sides of the coin:

Origin Country (Sending)

• Positive — Remittances: Migrants send billions of dollars back home to families, stimulating local markets and schools.

• Negative — Brain Drain: Loss of the smartest and most educated citizens (doctors, engineers, scientists) to wealthy nations.

• Demographic Impact: Skewed sex ratios in rural towns and an aging population left behind.

Destination Country (Receiving)

• Positive — Labor Influx: Fills vital shortages in agricultural harvesting, service industries, and high-tech STEM fields.

• Positive — Cultural Enrichment: Introduces diverse foods, festivals, architecture, and language enclaves.

• Negative — Social Friction: Pressure on public schools/clinics, and potential rise in anti-immigrant sentiment (Xenophobia).

Three Real-World Case Studies (Pick One for Your One-Pager)

For Zone 5 of your one-pager, you must illustrate an actual real-world migration stream. Here are three perfect options to choose from:

Option 1 • Forced International

The Syrian Civil War Refugee Crisis (2011–Present)

Push: Violent civil conflict, government barrel bombing, terror groups. Pull: Peace, human rights in Europe. Over 6.6 million refugees fled into Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan, and Germany. Obstacle: Dangerous Mediterranean boat crossings.

Option 2 • Voluntary Internal

The U.S. Great Migration (1916–1970)

Push: Jim Crow segregation laws, racial lynchings, boll weevil crop destruction in the rural South. Pull: WWI/WWII factory jobs and voting rights in Northern cities (Chicago, Detroit, New York). Over 6 million African Americans moved North.

Option 3 • Local Georgia Focus (Teacher Favorite!)

Clarkston, Georgia: "The Ellis Island of the South"

Located in DeKalb County right outside Atlanta. In the 1990s, the US government selected Clarkston for refugee resettlement due to cheap apartments, access to public transit (MARTA), and entry-level jobs in Atlanta. Today, refugees from 50+ nations live here, making it the most linguistically diverse square mile in America!
