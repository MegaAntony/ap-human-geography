Zone 2 • Theory Content Ravenstein's Laws

What to write for Ravenstein's Laws:

  • Distance Decay Principle: The majority of migrants travel only short distances within their own country. Interaction drops as distance grows.
  • Urban Gravitation: Migrants traveling longer distances disproportionately move to major commercial and industrial cities (Gravity Model).
  • Step Migration: Long-distance moves occur incrementally: farm ➔ village ➔ small town ➔ metropolis.
  • Rural to Urban Flow: Historically, rural agricultural populations are far more mobile than urban citizens.
  • Counter-Migration Streams: Every primary migration flow produces a compensating counter-stream (e.g., return migration).
  • Demographics: Historically, young single adult males migrate internationally for work, whereas families move internally.
Visual Drawing Tip: Sketch a downward curved line labeled "Distance Decay" next to a small sketch of a skyscraper city with arrows pointing toward it.
Zone 3 • Drivers Content 4-Category T-Chart

What to write for Push vs. Pull Factors:

1. Economic Factors (#1 Global Driver):
• Push: Factory closures, hyperinflation (Venezuela), lack of job prospects, deep poverty.
• Pull: Labor shortages, higher wage potential, industrial/tech opportunities.
2. Political Factors:
• Push: Armed civil war, totalitarian rule, religious/ethnic persecution, denial of liberties.
• Pull: Democratic stability, rule of law, fair trials, asylum protection.
3. Social / Cultural Factors:
• Push: Discrimination, rigid caste systems, gender oppression.
• Pull: Established ethnic enclaves, family sponsorship (Chain Migration), university hubs.
4. Environmental Factors:
• Push: Desertification (Sahel), crop failure (Irish Potato Famine), rising sea levels (Tuvalu).
• Pull: Temperate sunny climates (US Sunbelt), fertile land, safe freshwater access.
Spatial Nuance: Define Intervening Obstacle (mountain, desert, visa barrier) vs. Intervening Opportunity (finding a great job halfway to your destination).
Zone 4 • Core Vocab 6 Essential AP Terms

What to write in your Vocab Corner:

  • Refugee (UN 1951): A person forced to flee their home country across an international border due to well-founded fear of persecution or armed conflict.
  • Internally Displaced Person (IDP): A person forced to flee their home due to conflict or disaster who remains within their own nation's borders (lacks international treaty rights).
  • Asylum Seeker: A migrant who has crossed an international border and formally filed a legal petition for refugee status, awaiting adjudication.
  • Chain Migration: The migration of people to a specific community because relatives, friends, or members of the same nationality previously settled there.
  • Step Migration: Long-distance movement accomplished through a series of shorter stages or intermediary towns.
  • Transhumance: The seasonal cyclic movement of pastoralists and livestock between high summer mountain pastures and lowland winter valleys.
High-Yield Distinction: Mrs. Knight and the AP Exam always test whether you know that a Refugee crosses an international border while an IDP stays inside the country.
Zone 6 • Geographic Impact Origin vs. Destination

What to write for Consequences:

Impacts on Origin Country (Sending):

• Positive: Massive inflow of Remittances (cash sent back to families, fueling local retail and construction); reduced domestic unemployment.

• Negative: Brain Drain (loss of educated doctors, engineers, teachers); aging and skewed sex ratios left in rural communities.

Impacts on Destination Country (Receiving):

• Positive: Influx of young working-age labor offsets declining birth rates in aging societies (Stage 4/5 DTM); cultural diffusion and tax generation.

• Negative: Initial strains on schooling and public infrastructure; potential cultural friction, political polarization, or Xenophobia.

Option A • Forced International

The Syrian Civil War Refugee Crisis (2011–Present)

Causes: Violent armed civil conflict, government shelling, and extremist violence created the 21st century's largest forced displacement crisis.

• Origin: Syrian Arab Republic (Damascus, Aleppo).

• Destinations: Turkey (3.6M+), Jordan, Lebanon, Germany, Sweden.

• Scale: 6.6 Million external refugees; 6.7 Million internal IDPs.

• Obstacles: Mediterranean sea crossings, razor-wire EU border walls.

✏️ Sketch Map Guide: Draw Middle East map with arrows from Syria into Turkey and across Mediterranean to Germany.
Option B • Voluntary Internal

The U.S. Great Migration (1916–1970)

Causes: Over 6 million African Americans relocated from the rural South to urban industrial centers of the North, Midwest, and West.

• Push Factors: Jim Crow segregation laws, racial violence, sharecropping debt, and boll weevil crop destruction.

• Pull Factors: High-wage WWI/WWII factory jobs, industrial labor demand, voting rights, and cultural expression.

• Destinations: Chicago, Detroit, New York, Philadelphia.

• Impact: Harlem Renaissance, Chicago South Side cultural explosion.

✏️ Sketch Map Guide: Draw outline of US map with arrows rising from Alabama, Mississippi, and Georgia up toward Chicago, Detroit, and New York.
Option C • Georgia Local Focus

Clarkston, Georgia: "Ellis Island of the South"

Causes: In the 1990s, federal refugee agencies designated Clarkston (DeKalb County, GA) as a primary southeastern resettlement hub.

• Why Clarkston: Cheap apartment complexes, MARTA rapid transit, entry-level meatpacking and service jobs in Atlanta.

• Origin Nations: Over 50 countries including Somalia, Sudan, Bhutan, Afghanistan, Myanmar, and Syria.

• Landscape Impact: 1.4-square-mile town became the most linguistically diverse square mile in the United States.

Theoretical Spatial Models (AP High-Yield)

Connecting your one-pager to foundational AP geographic models guarantees maximum rubric points.

Wilbur Zelinsky's Migration Transition Model

Correlated to DTM

Geographer Wilbur Zelinsky proved that migration patterns shift systematically as a country advances through the Demographic Transition Model (DTM):

Stage 1 (Pre-Industrial): High CBR & High CDR. High daily or seasonal mobility (transhumance/pastoralism), but virtually no permanent international migration.
Stage 2 (Transitional - e.g., 19th Century Europe, modern Sub-Saharan Africa): Plunging CDR & soaring NIR cause overpopulation. High international emigration to wealthier nations and rapid internal rural-to-urban migration.
Stage 3 & 4 (Industrial & Modern - e.g., USA, UK, Germany): Low CBR & CDR. Destinations for international immigration. Primary internal movement is intraregional (cities to suburbs).

The Gravity Model of Spatial Interaction

Formula

Spatial Interaction = (Pop A × Pop B) ÷ (Distance)²

Core Concept: Larger population centers have greater economic gravitational pull on migrants, but distance acts as an exponential friction.

  • Why Atlanta attracts migrants from across Georgia rather than Macon or Augusta.
  • Why New York City attracts immigrants from around the world despite vast physical ocean distances.
  • Directly explains Ravenstein's law that long-distance migrants travel to large urban commercial capitals.
