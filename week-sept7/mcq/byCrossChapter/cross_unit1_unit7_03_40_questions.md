### Question 1 (STIM3-01)
- **Focus:** Topics 1.2 / 7.2 | **Skill:** Skill 2.A (Explain how map projections distort spatial relationships in global trade.)
- **AMSCO Reference:** AMSCO 2nd Ed (Palmer), Chapter 1, p. 26 (Review Question 1) & pp. 8-14
- **Visual Stimulus Graphic:** `[amsco_01_mercator_navigation.png]`
- **Stimulus Type:** Map Projection Diagram
> **Stimulus:** Questions 1 and 2 refer to the map projection shown below.

**Why is the cylindrical map projection shown in the image especially valuable for commercial transoceanic maritime trade navigation?**

- **(A)** Landmass areas across polar latitudes are displayed with absolute proportional fidelity relative to tropical continents.
- **(B)** **[CORRECT]** True rhumb lines of constant compass bearing plot as straight linear courses across the map, simplifying open ocean navigation.
- **(C)** Distortion of continental shapes is completely eliminated, allowing precise surface distance calculations without any scale adjustments.
- **(D)** It forces cargo ships to travel strictly along the equator, eliminating the need for satellite positioning technology at sea.

**Correct Answer:** **(B)**

**Explanation:**  
Refer to AMSCO Chapter 1, p. 26 (Question 1) & pp. 8-14: The Mercator projection is a cylindrical conformal projection. Its defining mathematical property is that lines of constant compass bearing (rhumb lines or loxodromes) appear as straight lines. Navigators can plot a straight line between ports and maintain a single compass bearing, making it the historic foundation of oceanic trade navigation.

**Distractor Rationales:**
- **Option (A):** Mercator severely exaggerates high-latitude landmasses (e.g., Greenland appearing as large as Africa), the opposite of proportional area fidelity.
- **Option (C):** No flat map can eliminate distortion entirely; Mercator preserves shape locally but severely distorts area and distance at high latitudes.
- **Option (D):** The projection does not force ships to travel along the equator; it is used for navigation across all oceans and latitudes.

**Which major cartographic limitation of this projection has drawn criticism from development geographers analyzing global inequality?**

- **(A)** **[CORRECT]** It severely exaggerates the landmass area of high-latitude wealthy nations while drastically shrinking equatorial developing continents.
- **(B)** It inverts the Northern and Southern hemispheres, placing Antarctica at the top of classroom instructional maps worldwide.
- **(C)** It completely erases national political boundaries, preventing geographers from measuring gross domestic product across sovereign states.

**Correct Answer:** **(A)**

**Explanation:**

### Question 9 (STIM3-09)
- **Focus:** Topics 1.4 / 7.2 | **Skill:** Skill 2.B (Explain how Weber's Least Cost Theory balances raw material and market friction.)

- **Stimulus Type:** Economic Model Diagram
> **Stimulus:** Questions 9 and 10 refer to Alfred Weber's Least Cost Theory model shown below.

**In Alfred Weber's Least Cost Theory diagram, what spatial condition compels a production facility to locate directly adjacent to Raw Material 1?**

- **(A)** Raw Material 1 is weightless and ubiquitously accessible across every parcel of land throughout the entire geographic region.
- **(B)** **[CORRECT]** Raw Material 1 experiences substantial weight loss during fabrication, whereas other inputs are ubiquitous and readily available at market.
- **(C)** Government industrial subsidies mandate that all fabrication facilities must construct headquarters atop high-cost urban retail real estate.
- **(D)** Finished goods are ten times heavier and more perishable than the unprocessed rocks extracted from the raw material excavation quarry.

**Correct Answer:** **(B)**

**Explanation:**  
Refer to AMSCO Chapter 18, pp. 456-458: Alfred Weber's Least Cost Theory posits that manufacturing location is determined primarily by minimizing transportation costs. If Raw Material 1 is 'bulk-reducing' (weight-losing, like copper ore or coal) and localized, shipping the unrefined rock to market is cost-prohibitive. If other inputs are ubiquitous (like air or water), the plant pulls directly to the weight-losing raw material site.

**Distractor Rationales:**
- **Option (A):** If Raw Material 1 were weightless and ubiquitous, it would exert zero spatial pull toward its source.
- **Option (C):** Weber's model is based on free-market cost optimization (transport, labor, agglomeration), not government real estate mandates.
- **Option (D):** If finished goods were ten times heavier, production would be bulk-gaining and locate at the market, not at the raw material source.

**How does the substitution principle modify Weber's classic transport-cost minimization model in contemporary high-tech secondary manufacturing?**
